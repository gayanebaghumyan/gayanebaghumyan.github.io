Research

Ambiguity and Racial Discrimination Job Market Paper

[PDF]

In this study, I ask whether ambiguity in a decision environment can provide moral cover for racial discrimination. The study is motivated by the idea that discriminatory preferences may not be expressed when people cannot easily justify acting on them. Ambiguity can provide a plausible non-discriminatory reason for biased behavior. I conducted a preregistered, incentivized experiment with 4,327 White U.S. participants who allocated real monetary bonuses to actual recipients. The experiment varied whether the ambiguously described payoff option benefited a Black recipient or a White recipient. This design enables me to distinguish between a general tendency to avoid ambiguously framed options and the selective use of ambiguity as a justification for discrimination. The results do not support the hypothesis that ambiguity provides moral cover for discrimination at the population level. Participants choose the ambiguously framed option more often when it benefits the Black recipient than when it benefits the White recipient, the opposite of what the selective use of ambiguity against Black recipients would predict. Overall, the evidence is more consistent with a general tendency to avoid ambiguously framed options than with ambiguity serving as moral cover for racial discrimination.

Sexual-Orientation Discrimination and Biological Attributions: Experimental Evidence from Russia

CERGE-EI Working Paper Series No. 762

[SSRN]

In this paper, I investigate whether information about the biological causes of homosexuality influences discriminatory behavior. Attribution theory suggests that perceiving sexual orientation as innate—rather than chosen—should reduce discrimination, since people tend to judge others less harshly for traits they cannot control. I conducted an online experiment with a representative sample of Russian adults. Participants were presented with scientific evidence supporting biological explanations for homosexuality, and discrimination was measured using incentivized allocation tasks. Contrary to the pre-registered hypothesis, however, exposure to information supporting biological explanations for homosexuality did not attenuate discrimination. Instead, the treatment increased discriminatory behavior. One possible interpretation is that information that conflicts with prior beliefs triggers cognitive dissonance, or irritation, particularly among participants whose views are inconsistent with biological explanations of homosexuality. Another possibility is that the information made sexual minorities seem fundamentally different. Overall, this chapter shows that information provision is not necessarily a straightforward solution to discrimination; in certain contexts, particularly intolerant ones, it may exacerbate the behavior it is intended to reduce.

Religious Identity and Sexual-Orientation Discrimination: Experimental Evidence from Russia

Draft available on request

This paper examines whether the salience of religious identity causally affects discrimination based on sexual orientation. Religion is often associated with negative attitudes toward sexual minorities, but establishing a causal link is difficult. Religious affiliation and religiosity are shaped by family background, culture, and personal values, making it hard to isolate their independent effects on discriminatory behavior. To address this challenge, I conduct an online experiment in which I employ a priming technique to activate religious identity before participants make incentivized allocation decisions and answer questions about their support for LGB-related policies. The results show significant sexual-orientation discrimination: participants allocate less money to same-sex profiles than to different-sex profiles. Additionally, more religious participants show less support for LGB-related policies. Men, older respondents, and politically conservative individuals also exhibit greater discrimination. In the causal analysis, however, the religious prime shows no statistically robust average effect on either allocation decisions or policy preferences. Exploratory heterogeneity analysis suggests that the prime may have more negative effects among moderately religious respondents than among the most religious respondents, particularly for policy attitudes, but these results are sensitive to multiple hypothesis correction. The chapter therefore adds causal evidence to a literature that has largely relied on correlations, while also showing the limits of this particular priming intervention and pointing to the need for further work in other religious contexts, with larger samples and stronger priming interventions.
